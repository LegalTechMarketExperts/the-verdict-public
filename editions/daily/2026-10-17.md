# Paying law firms for their emails puts privilege on the table

Above the Law reports that a technology company is paying law firms for access to their emails, and client privilege is the stake.

Above the Law reports that a technology company has launched a programme to pay law firms for their emails. The company is Mode, a Chicago consumer-data business, through a scheme it calls BizBuyData. Mode says it buys de-identified copies of email, chat, documents and billing records from firms of six or more staff, at an indicative $100k to $5m, with legal one of several eligible sectors. Mode's chief executive says AI labs cannot scrape this data, which he presents as the point of the scheme. No participating firm is named.

Our read: the pitch is scarcity. Data the labs cannot scrape is worth more to them. It is also the category where client confidentiality and privilege decide whether a firm may sell at all. One company's offer is not a market, and no firm is named as taking part. Before any client email leaves the building, ask who decides what counts as privileged.

**A cyber incident reaches US bankruptcy courts.** The ABA Journal reports that a cybersecurity incident at BAE Systems, whose US subsidiary runs the Bankruptcy Noticing Center for the federal judiciary, has halted bankruptcy notice production for every US bankruptcy court. The Administrative Office of the US Courts confirmed the incident on 15 October; the system has been offline since 9 October and no restoration date has been given. Our read: dependency chains run further than most incident plans. Ask which of your filing and notice routes fail when a supplier you never contracted with has an incident.

**Eight state attorneys general sue OpenAI.** Troutman Pepper Locke reports that eight state attorneys general sued OpenAI on 14 October 2026, filed separately in each state's own courts, over ChatGPT safety claims and consumer protection. The states are Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia. Separately, commentator Edward Lee has launched a tracker that counts 119 suits against OpenAI, covering copyright suits worldwide plus US tort and state AG actions, and excluding employment claims. Our read: treat 119 as direction, not a figure. Ask each AI supplier which of its underlying models faces active claims.
